A picture book cover has to do something most interior spreads don't: work at two very different scales at once. On a bookshop shelf it's competing with dozens of other spines and covers for a split-second of attention. On a retailer's website, it might be rendered at 120 pixels wide.

That constraint drives almost every palette decision I make on a cover.

## Silhouette first, colour second

Before I choose a single colour, I check the piece in pure black and white. If the silhouette doesn't read — if you can't tell what's happening from shape alone — no palette will fix it. Once the silhouette works, colour is there to set mood, not to do the storytelling.

## Limiting the palette on purpose

Covers get a much tighter palette than interior spreads. Usually three or four dominant colours, deliberately chosen to contrast against whatever else might be nearby on a shelf — a lot of children's covers lean warm, so a cooler, moodier palette can actually stand out more by doing the opposite.

## Testing small

The very last thing I do before calling a cover finished is shrink it down to postage-stamp size on my screen. If the title and the central character are still legible at that size, it's done. If not, back to the palette (or the composition) it goes.
