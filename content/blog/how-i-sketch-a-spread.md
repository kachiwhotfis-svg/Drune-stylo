Every picture book spread I've ever finished started as something genuinely ugly. Scribbled proportions, a horizon line in the wrong place, a character whose head is three sizes too big. That's not a failure — that's the point of a thumbnail.

At this stage I'm only asking one question: **where does the reader's eye land first, and where does it go next?** Colour, texture, and clean linework don't matter yet. If the thumbnail doesn't work as a rough grey blob, no amount of polish later will save it.

## Why I keep the ugly version

I used to be embarrassed by my roughs. Now I keep every single one, because:

- They're the fastest way to test a composition before investing hours in it
- They show a client or art director the *idea* without the noise of finished rendering
- They're proof, months later, of how much a piece actually changed

## The clean-up pass

Once a thumbnail earns its place, it gets a clean-up pass — not final line art yet, just confident enough lines that I can see the piece clearly. This is where proportions get fixed and the composition gets locked in.

Only after that does it get to be line art, and only after *that* does it get to be colour. Skipping steps is how you end up redrawing an entire spread at 80% finished — ask me how I know.
