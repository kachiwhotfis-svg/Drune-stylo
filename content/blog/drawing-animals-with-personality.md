The biggest trap in drawing animal characters for children's books is over-humanising them. Give an animal a full human wardrobe, human hands, and a human posture, and you don't get a bear anymore — you get a person wearing a bear costume, and something about it always feels a little off.

The better approach, at least for me, is to pick **one** human gesture or expression and let the rest of the character stay completely, believably animal.

## One gesture is enough

A dragon holding a teacup with just two claws, slightly too delicately, tells you everything about his personality in one image — fussy, a little formal, secretly delighted to have company. He doesn't need a waistcoat or spectacles to read as a character. In fact, adding those things would dilute the one gesture doing all the work.

## Let the anatomy stay honest

Wings still work like wings. Ears still sit where ears sit. If a bear is going to hold an umbrella, I think about how a bear's paw actually moves before I think about how a hand would hold it. The character earns its personality by staying anatomically believable and *then* doing something charming — not by cheating the anatomy for the sake of the gesture.

## Expression lives in a few key shapes

Across a whole book, I try to keep a character's expressive range down to changes in just a few shapes — the brow line, the mouth, maybe the ear angle. Keeping that vocabulary small means the character stays consistent and recognisable, spread after spread, even as their mood changes completely.
